P.01 · PRODUCTION

Activv Connect

A full web and mobile platform connecting athletes to local sports activities, designed, coded, and shipped from first commit to launch. Auth, discovery with filters, activity management, and an admin dashboard.

Next.jsSupabaseVercel

THE PROBLEM

Casual and competitive athletes have no reliable way to find people to play with. Pickup games live in group chats and word of mouth, so a player who moves to a new city, or simply wants a tennis match on a Tuesday evening, has no way to discover who is playing, where, and at what skill level. Activv Connect was founded to solve exactly that: a single hub where athletes find, create, and join activities.

MY ROLE

I was brought in as the developer and built the product from the ground up, taking it from an empty repository to a working platform across web, iOS, Android, and tablet. There was no existing codebase, no schema, and no infrastructure. I made the architecture decisions, wrote the frontend and the backend, designed the database, and deployed it.

  • Registration and authentication. Sign up and login flows with session handling, backed by Supabase auth.

  • Activity creation. A host flow capturing sport, title, description, date and time, location, skill level, and maximum player count.
  • Activity detail and participation. Viewing the roster of attendees, joining, and leaving, with capacity limits enforced so a game never overfills.
  • Admin dashboard. Internal tooling for the team to manage users, manage activities, and moderate content.

The frontend is Next.js, chosen so one codebase serves the responsive web app and the mobile and tablet experiences. P.02 · CAPSTONE · 2026

Eye Tracking Interface

Computer vision eye tracking with an emergency alert module. Users with disabilities trigger notifications using only their gaze, no hands needed. Built with a team of five.

PythonOpenCV

THE PROBLEM

For someone with severe motor impairment, calling for help can be the hardest thing in the room. A button on the wall is useless if you cannot reach it, and voice control fails if speech is affected. What almost always remains under a person's control is the eyes. If a camera can read where someone is looking and for how long, then a deliberate gaze becomes a switch, and a switch becomes a way to call for help.

THE SYSTEM

A standard webcam feeds a computer vision pipeline that locates the face, isolates the eye region, and tracks the pupil to estimate gaze direction. That gaze position drives an on screen interface, so a user can select targets with their eyes alone. The design constraint throughout was no hands and no voice: every interaction had to be possible with gaze only.

MY CONTRIBUTION

The project was built by a team of five, coordinated through GitHub, and split into three components: the eye tracking engine, the graphical interface, and the alert system. I built the emergency alert module, the piece that turns a sustained gaze into a real world notification, and I worked on integrating the three components into a single working system. Integration was its own problem: the tracker, the interface, and the alert logic each worked in isolation long before they worked together.

WHY IT MATTERS

Accessibility software is judged by a harsher standard than most. A false trigger is an unnecessary emergency, and a missed trigger is worse. Building the alert path meant thinking about the cost of both failure modes rather than optimising a single accuracy number.

P.03 · PUBLISHED · IC3T 2023

EEG Signal Classification

Collected motor imagery EEG from human subjects on clinical equipment. Real data, not public datasets. Then benchmarked ML classifiers, with results published at the IC3T International Conference.

Pythonscikit-learn

THE PROBLEM

Motor imagery is the act of mentally rehearsing a movement without performing it. Imagining a left hand movement lights up nearly the same motor cortex circuits as actually moving it, and that electrical activity is measurable on the scalp. If a machine can reliably tell which movement a person is imagining, a paralysed user can steer a wheelchair or a cursor by thought alone. That is the promise of a Brain Computer Interface, and the bottleneck is classification accuracy.

WHY THE EXISTING APPROACH FALLS SHORT

The established literature leans on classical machine learning, principally Support Vector Machines and Linear Discriminant Analysis. Those methods carry three limits that matter for this signal: they assume near linear separability, while EEG is deeply non linear; they are sensitive to hyperparameter tuning; and, treating each sample independently, they struggle to capture the temporal dependencies that define a brainwave. Our thesis was that a deep model could learn those patterns directly instead of having them hand engineered.

THE DATA

Signals were captured on an EMOTIV EPOC+ 14 channel electroencephalogram sampling at 128 Hz through a 16 bit converter, across the standard electrode sites AF3, F7, F3, FC5, T7, P7, O1, O2, P8, T8, FC6, F4, F8, and AF4. Subjects ran a twenty five minute protocol built from repeated cycles: an arrow to the right cued an imagined rightward motor action, an arrow to the left cued a leftward one, and a circle cued no action at all. Varying the scenarios deliberately was what let the model generalise rather than memorise one session.

SIGNAL PROCESSING

Each reading arrives as a 128 by 14 matrix. Since the informative brainwaves sit between 0 and 30 Hz, we applied a Fast Fourier Transform across that band, collapsing each reading into a 30 by 14 matrix in the frequency domain. Computing weighted and arithmetic means per wave then produced a 14 by 4 by 2 tensor holding the mean values for every channel across the delta, theta, alpha, and beta bands. Classes came out balanced at 3,840 samples each.

THE MODELS

Rather than defend one architecture, we built three and let the numbers decide. A 1D Convolutional Neural Network (Conv1D layers, max pooling at stride 2, flatten, dropout at 0.25 for overfitting, dense softmax output), an LSTM, and a GRU, all trained with categorical crossentropy and the Adam optimiser over 10 epochs at batch size 32, on an 80/20 split with standardised features.

THE RESULT

The convolutional model won decisively, and by a margin large enough to be interesting rather than incremental. The recurrent architectures, despite being the textbook choice for sequence data, landed barely above the noise floor here. The lesson was that after the FFT the discriminative information lives in the spatial arrangement across frequency bands and electrodes, which convolution is built to exploit, rather than in long range time dependence, which is what LSTM and GRU are built for. Choosing the architecture that matches the shape of the transformed data mattered more than choosing the more sophisticated one.

OUTCOME

The work was written up as a major project at Kakatiya Institute of Technology and Science under Smt. V. Gouthami, and the results were published at the IC3T International Conference in 2023.

P.04 · COMPUTER VISION

Object Detection for the Visually Impaired

A detection model that scans the scene and speaks. Audio cues and synthesized speech describe identified objects out loud, helping visually impaired users navigate their surroundings.

TensorFlowOpenCVPython

THE PROBLEM

A white cane tells you that something is there. It does not tell you what. A visually impaired person navigating an unfamiliar space has to reconstruct the room from touch and sound, and the gap between detecting an obstacle and understanding it is exactly where a camera and a model can help.

THE SYSTEM

A camera feed runs through an object detection model that identifies what is in the scene, and the recognised objects are converted into audio cues and spoken descriptions, so the environment is described out loud as the user moves through it. The output channel is the whole point: a detection the user cannot perceive is not a detection.

MY CONTRIBUTION

I worked on building the detection model itself, and I added the speech feedback layer so the system could describe identified objects aloud rather than only labelling them on a screen that the user cannot see. Built with TensorFlow and OpenCV in Python.

THE INTERESTING CONSTRAINT

Assistive detection is not really a computer vision problem, it is an information rate problem. A model that announces every object in view produces a wall of speech that is worse than silence. The design question is what to say, when, and in what order, and that question is not answered by raising the model's accuracy.

P.06 · APPLIED ML

Heart Disease Prediction

A clinical risk classifier trained on patient cardiology data, benchmarked across four algorithms, and shipped as a working desktop application that returns a diagnosis with a confidence score.

scikit-learnPythonTkinter

THE PROBLEM

Cardiovascular disease kills more people than anything else on earth, and much of that toll is a detection problem rather than a treatment problem. The clinical measurements that hint at heart disease are cheap and routine: age, blood pressure, cholesterol, resting ECG, maximum heart rate, exercise induced angina. The question is whether a model reading those thirteen numbers can flag the patients who need a closer look.

THE DATA

A cardiology dataset of 1,025 patient records with thirteen clinical features and a binary diagnosis target. Features span demographics (age, sex), symptoms (chest pain type, exercise induced angina), vitals (resting blood pressure, serum cholesterol, fasting blood sugar), and diagnostics (resting ECG results, maximum heart rate achieved, ST depression, slope of the peak exercise ST segment, number of major vessels, thalassemia). Features were standardised before training, and the fitted scaler was saved alongside the model so that inference applies exactly the same transform as training.

THE MODELS

I did not settle for one algorithm. I trained a Random Forest, then a Support Vector Machine, then a Gradient Boosting ensemble, evaluating each properly rather than trusting a single train/test split.

EVALUATION, DONE PROPERLY

  • Confusion matrix. The SVM produced 91 true negatives, 99 true positives, 9 false positives, and 6 false negatives. Those six matter most: a missed heart disease is the expensive error, which is why recall on the disease class (0.94) is the number I watch rather than raw accuracy.
  • Classification report. Precision 0.94 and 0.92, recall 0.91 and 0.94, F1 0.92 and 0.93 across the two classes, so performance is balanced rather than propped up by one class.
  • ROC curve and AUC to assess the classifier across every decision threshold, not just the default one.
  • Five fold cross validation to confirm the score was a property of the model and not an accident of one lucky split.
  • Hyperparameter tuning and feature selection, using feature importance to see which clinical signals the model actually leans on.

SHIPPED, NOT SHELVED

The trained model and scaler were serialised and wrapped in a desktop application where a clinician enters the thirteen values and gets back an immediate verdict, a confidence percentage, and a probability chart across both classes. Same instinct as the cloud API project: a model nobody can run is not finished.
